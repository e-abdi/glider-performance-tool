Study Design Tool

Draw a study area, choose a simple transect design, and estimate survey distance and nominal mission duration. This prototype is intended for first-order study design rather than operational mission planning.

Study requirements

Use the polygon tool on the map to define one study area. Edit the polygon at any time; the design will update when you regenerate it.

Survey geometry

A butterfly flies two diagonals that cross near the middle of the area, joined at each end into a figure 8, and repeats the loop. It is sized to fit inside the study area, so transect spacing does not apply.

0° = north-south transects; 90° = east-west.

Mission assumptions

Angle from horizontal. Used with target depth to estimate the horizontal distance of one down-and-up dive cycle.

Historical depth-averaged currents

The typical current over the study area, averaged from the surface to the target depth (or to the seabed, if shallower), for the months of the deployment. These are historical values, not a forecast and not what a glider will measure on the day: they come from the Copernicus GLORYS12 reanalysis, an ocean model that assimilates observations (satellite sea level and temperature, Argo floats and other in-situ profiles), averaged month by month over 1993–2016. That averaging smooths away tides, eddies and storms. The survey estimate below flies the route through these currents.

How the current estimate is made
  • Eastward and northward velocity (uo, vo) on GLORYS12's 1/12° grid and 50 depth levels, monthly climatology over 1993–2016, read directly from Copernicus Marine's public ARCO store for the grid cells around the study area.
  • DAC in each grid cell is the thickness-weighted mean velocity from the surface to the target depth; where the model seabed is shallower, it is averaged to the seabed instead.
  • Area statistics use the grid cells inside the study polygon (all cells in its bounding box if the polygon is smaller than one cell), pooled across the deployment months.
  • How these currents change the survey's speed, distance and duration is explained under Survey estimate.
  • GLORYS12 is a model run that is continually corrected towards observations (data assimilation), so it is neither raw measurements nor a free-running model. Its monthly climatological means smooth out tides, eddies, storms and inter-annual variability. Currents on a given day can be considerably stronger than these values — treat them as a typical background, not a bound.

Currents: E.U. Copernicus Marine Service Information, Global Ocean Physics Reanalysis (GLOBAL_MULTIYEAR_PHY_001_030, monthly climatology), doi:10.48670/moi-00021.

Survey estimate

First-order estimates from the survey geometry and the historical currents above. The current along each part of the track is added to the glider's speed where it follows the glider and subtracted where it opposes it; a cross-current makes the glider crab, which also costs speed. Dive behaviour, bathymetry, payload power, navigation, launch/recovery and operational safety margins are not included.

Calculation assumptions
  • Transects are generated at the selected compass orientation and clipped to the study polygon.
  • For the lawnmower option, transect segments are alternated and connected in sequence; connector distance is included.
  • The butterfly is four legs: two diagonals that cross near the middle of the area, and the two ends that join them. Each corner is placed as far out as the area allows in its quadrant either side of the track orientation, then pulled in until every leg stays inside the area. Its duration is for one loop; the number of loops that fit in the deployment is shown with it.
  • Still-water travel rate is speed in knots × 1.852 km/nmi × 24 h/day.
  • With currents, the route is walked in short steps and each step takes the historical current at that spot (nearest model cell). The glider is assumed to crab into the current to hold its track: ground speed = along-track current + √(speed² − cross-track current²). A current along the track therefore adds to or subtracts from the glider's speed directly; a cross-current only slows it.
  • Distance through water is how far the glider moves relative to the water: its speed through water × the time taken. It is longer than the track where the current opposes the glider and shorter where it helps, and it is what sets the number of dive cycles.
  • Where the cross-track current is stronger than the glider, or the result is no forward progress, the track cannot be held; that distance is left out of the duration and reported instead. If more than 5 % of the track cannot be held, the duration is shown as not feasible.
  • Duration with currents is the average over the deployment months of the duration flown in each month's currents; the worst month is shown beside it. Surfacing dots use the average current over those months.
  • Dive-cycle horizontal distance is approximated as 2 × target depth / tan(glide angle), assuming a symmetric straight-line descent and ascent.
  • Map dots represent estimated surfacing locations, not subsurface profile positions or commanded waypoints. With currents they are bunched up where the glider is slowed and spread out where it is helped along.

The mission over the seafloor

Draws the planned track as it would actually be flown, against real bathymetry for the study area. Dive depth can follow the seafloor instead of being fixed, which is what a pilot would really do over a shelf break or a canyon. Once currents are estimated above, the depth-averaged current is drawn as arrows on the sea surface over the dives: longer for stronger, red where it is faster than the glider.
